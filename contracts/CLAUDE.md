## The deployed pool is not redeployable

`deployment.json:pool` → `0xeBBA114d9870c98250239aCaFbcccc4dA09AF1CA` on Monad
testnet. Its value is its **history**: audit requests registered at blocks that
precede their answers, and two requests that have been open — provably
unanswered — since a named block. A redeploy throws that away and the project's
central claim with it.

So: fixes to this source that are not deployed are **documentation of a known
gap**, and `SUMMARY.md` lists them under that heading. Currently absent from the
deployed bytecode: `TREE_CAPACITY`/`EXIT_LEAVES`, the ERC-3643/ERC-1400 surface,
and the circuit's dummy-input guard. Verified absent by selector probing, not by
assumption. If you add another, add it to that list in the same commit.

## Things that will bite you

**`claimHash` is what makes a disclosure mean anything.** The threshold circuit
proves "balance ≥ T" for a prover-chosen `T`, and `T = 2^64-1` always has a
witness. On its own that proves close to nothing. The auditor pins `T` in the
request, `claimHash` binds it, and the contract refuses an answer to a different
question. This was found as a live vulnerability (H-1) — two agents found it
independently and a third built the cheating proof.

**`kind == KIND_EXACT` must carry `claim == bytes32(0)`**, and `claimHash`
returns `bytes32(0)` for it. The exact path's subject *is* the question.

**An answered audit cannot be retracted.** `auditAnswered[contextHash] != 0` is
final. One row on chain carries a `correction` field for exactly this reason —
an aggregate was answered over 3 of 4 positions and called complete.

**`_requireKnown` proves a commitment was deposited, not that it is unspent.**
The chain structurally cannot distinguish a live position from a retired one,
because that is the privacy property. Any "complete" claim rests on the
auditor's enumeration, not on the proof.

**A `contextHash` of 0 is refused** — it is the entry-binding domain and 0 is
the "no such audit" sentinel.

## Verifiers

Generated by snarkjs from `circuits/`. All six deployed verifiers were checked
byte-for-byte against the committed verifying keys in their bytecode. If you
rebuild a circuit you must redeploy its verifier and re-check — a verifier
carrying a stale key accepts nothing, loudly, which is the good failure. The bad
one is a verifier for a *different* circuit with the same public-signal count.
